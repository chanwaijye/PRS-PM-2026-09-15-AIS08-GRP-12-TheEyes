Scene 1. One camera loses the cube.

ON SCREEN: Live take, before any title. The phone's picture of the table beside the console. One cube on the table. A card slides in between the cube and one camera.

NOTE: the take is demo_cold_d (10 Oct 2026, 21:50).

This is a red cube on a table, with two depth cameras looking at it.

Now I block one of the cameras. (pause)

That camera has lost the cube completely. But the system still reports the cube, in the same place, and it adds one new fact: the cube is occluded from that camera.

Seeing with two cameras, and knowing when not to trust one of them. That is this project.


Scene 2. Title.

ON SCREEN: Title card: The Eyes - Multi-View Perception and Scene Understanding for Robot Manipulation. Chan Wai Jye, Group 12. Then the photo of the rig.

Hi, I'm Wai Jye from Group 12, this is a solo project called The Eyes. It is the perception part for a tabletop robot. Two depth cameras detect the objects, estimate each one's six D-O-F pose, combine the two views into one answer, and turn that answer into facts a planner can use. The code was built with Claude Code, under my design and review.


Scene 3. Why one camera is not enough.

ON SCREEN: Slide: one camera, an object hidden behind another. Then two cameras and two estimates that do not meet. Then the three questions.

A planner does not work with pixels. It works with statements, like "the cube is on the table". In my earlier practice module, The Planner, those statements came from a simulator, which always knows the truth. A real robot has to get them from cameras.

With one camera, anything hidden behind something else simply disappears. A second camera can see it again, but now every object has two estimates that never quite agree. So there are three questions. Which estimates are the same object? When two views disagree, which one is wrong? And how do poses become facts reliable enough to plan with?


Scene 4. The rig.

ON SCREEN: Phone footage of the rig: the wide shot, each camera close up, the calibration board on the table, the three printed objects, the marker trays. Caption over the camera close-ups: each camera is selected by serial number, never by the order it was plugged in.

This is the rig. Two Intel RealSense D four-three-five i cameras on tripods, about 0.74 metres apart, with about 80 degrees between their views. The software pairs their frames by timestamp, never more than 17 milliseconds apart.

The cameras are calibrated against this board, to about 0.3 pixels, and registered again at the start of every session, because the rig drifts even when nobody touches it.

The three objects are 3D printed from the same meshes the pose estimator uses. The grey marker trays are only for measuring ground truth. The pipeline never looks at them.


Scene 5. The pipeline.

ON SCREEN: The system diagram, one block lit at a time. At the last paragraph, the console replaying a recorded scene, with a label on each panel: camera views, table from above, scene graph, text sent to the planner. Caption on the console part: REPLAY of a recorded scene.

Here is the whole system: one C plus plus process.

The top row runs once for each camera. A detector finds the objects: YOLO v8 nano with a four-channel input, colour plus depth, trained in this project on about 40,000 synthetic frames from Isaac Sim. Then FoundationPose, pretrained and frozen, estimates each object's six D-O-F pose from its CAD mesh.

The bottom row runs once for each pair of frames. These are the project's core modules, written from first principles in C plus plus, with no fusion or scene-graph library. Every pose is moved into one shared table frame and checked against what the two depth images actually measured. Estimates of the same object are matched across the views. If the two views disagree by more than 50 millimetres or 30 degrees, they are not averaged: one is kept. Otherwise they are combined, weighted by confidence, and smoothed over time with a Kalman filter for each object.

The last stage turns geometry into four kinds of fact: on, in, clear, and occluded from. This console shows all of it at once.

ON SCREEN: One continuous live take. The console fills the frame; the phone's picture of the table and my hands sits in a corner, in step with it. Caption for the whole scene: LIVE on the rig, 10 Oct 2026, one take, not replayed. The measured speed of the take stays on screen as a caption: 12.1 frame pairs a second over the take, 10 with three objects. Three more captions, each at its moment. When the bottle's facts appear: "The third fact is wrong: nothing hides the bottle. Fixed the next day. See: What it still gets wrong." At the first freeze: "Freeze: the pose estimator is finding a new object, about 1 second." When the card stands before the front camera: "The boxes on the card are false cubes, from its red print. The depth check refuses them."

NOTE: the take is demo_live_a (10 Oct 2026, 21:33), and the words follow it beat by beat; the time of each beat is in docs/OCTOBER_PLAN.md, 10 Oct. The console file holds one frame for each processed pair, so it is retimed before the voice is cut to it (docs/VIDEO_PLAN.md, section 7).

Now a live run. The small picture is my phone, filming the table at the same moment as the screen. Nothing here is replayed.

The table is empty, and the only fact says so: the table is clear. (pause)

I put down the bottle. (pause) Both cameras detect it, and a moment later the facts appear: the bottle is on the table, and nothing is on top of it.

The bowl. (pause) And the cube. (pause) Three objects, each on the table.

Now the cube goes into the bowl. (pause) The fact changes from "on the table" to "in the bowl". I take it out, (pause) and it is on the table again.

Watch the three counters at the bottom left: the front camera alone, the other camera alone, and the fused result. I stand a card between the cube and one camera. (pause) Its count drops. The fused count does not, and a new fact says which camera cannot see the cube. (pause)

Now the other camera. (pause) Its count goes to zero, and the fused count is still three. (pause)

I take the bottle away, (pause) and its facts go with it.

With three objects on the table this runs at 10 frame pairs a second, and about four fifths of that time is the pose estimator.


Scene 7. How it is measured.

ON SCREEN: A ground-truth still with the tray markers outlined. Then the sixteen scenes laid out by level. Then one line: 16 scenes, 300 frame pairs each, recorded once, replayed for every comparison. Then the photo of the cube in the bowl, from above.

A demo is not a measurement, so this is how I measured it.

Each object sits in a printed tray that carries markers. The markers give the true pose, and the two cameras agree on it to within a millimetre and a half.

I recorded 16 scenes at four levels: an object alone, three objects among clutter, an object half hidden from one camera, and an object fully hidden from one camera. Each is 300 frame pairs, recorded once, and every method is replayed on exactly the same frames. Two more recordings have the cube inside the bowl. In both, the "in" fact is reported on all 300 frame pairs.

The score is A-D-D-S, the distance between the estimated surface and the true one. A frame counts as correct when that is within 10 percent of the object's diameter: for the cube, under 9 millimetres.


Scene 8. The result: a hidden object is still found.

ON SCREEN: Replays of two fully hidden scenes, one blocked from each side, each beside its row of the table. Caption: REPLAY of a recorded scene. Then the table of all four fully hidden scenes, with the share of the object the blocked camera sees in each: 0%, 0%, 6% and 2%. Then the cube-alone rows, with the plain-average row beside them.

The main result. In four scenes the object is fully hidden from one camera. In every one, the blocked camera finds it on zero frames out of 300. The fused output has it on all 300, within 2 to 4 millimetres of the truth.

A single camera does as well only when it happens to be the one that is not blocked, and across these scenes that changes. No single camera covers all four. The fused system does.

With nothing hidden, two views are also more accurate than one. On the cube alone, each camera by itself is about 3 millimetres off. Fused, it is 1.7.

But how you combine them matters. Simply averaging the two poses gives 22 millimetres on the same cube, worse than either camera, because a cube looks the same from several directions and the two cameras pick different ones. The fusion module compares orientations with that symmetry taken out.


Scene 9. When fusion believed the wrong camera.

ON SCREEN: Replay of the half-hidden bottle under the old rule: the fused bottle standing at the cover. Then a diagram: the other camera's line of sight passing through the place the bottle was claimed to be. Then the same scene under the new rule. Small print: the occlusion level of the 90 millimetre recording is not certified by the visibility tool; the 34 millimetre one is.

Now the part that failed first, and taught me the most.

Here the bottle is half hidden from one camera. That camera sees only a strip of it and places the whole bottle at the cover, 7 to 12 centimetres from the truth. And the pose estimator scores that wrong pose higher than the correct one from the other camera.

The first rule for a disagreement was to keep the higher score. So fusion believed the wrong camera: 34 millimetres of error in one recording and 90 in another, when the good camera alone was at 3. Fusion made the answer worse.

The fix does not use the score. It uses the other camera's depth image. If the bottle really were where the first camera says, the other camera could not see through that space. If it measures a surface behind it, the estimate is thrown out.

With that check, the two recordings go from 34 and 90 millimetres to 3.1 and 3.2. The other 18 recordings do not change, and in 4,800 frame pairs the check never rejected a correct estimate.


Scene 10. Objects that are not there.

ON SCREEN: A console frame with a false object boxed in a camera view: the tray labelled as a bowl, a cube on the printed card. Then the count before and after.

The second surprise was objects that are not there. The detector has only seen synthetic images, and on the real table it takes a grey tray for a bowl, or the red print on a card for a cube. The pose estimator then fits a model to it, with a high score.

In the first seven scenes, false objects were reported 2,771 times. They had one thing in common: none of them rests on anything. They float, or sit under the table.

So the scene graph now reports an object only if something supports it. That took 2,771 false reports down to 1, without losing a real one. And in the three clutter scenes, recorded afterwards, none of 6,210 facts names an object that is not there.


Scene 11. What it still gets wrong.

ON SCREEN: The heading "What it still gets wrong" stays up for the whole scene. One picture for each item: the dim frame; the bottle with its axes pointing the wrong way; the third clutter scene; the live take's frame with the wrong fact outlined; the detector table with and without depth.

The real room is darker than the synthetic training images. Below a certain brightness one camera loses the cube completely, so every session starts with a light check.

The bottle is sometimes read back to front, half a turn from the truth. The depth check cannot catch that, because a bottle turned around fills almost the same space, and the A-D-D-S score barely shows it.

In one of the three clutter scenes the cube, small and far from one camera, lands outside its threshold. The other two objects in that scene are fine.

In the live run, the occluded-from fact was wrong about the bottle. That is fixed now; the take is from the day before. But a camera whose detector misses an object still reports it as hidden.

And on synthetic data the depth channel did not help the detector: the M-A-P is 0.957 with it and 0.957 without.

[VERIFY: one or two sentences on the detector after it is fine-tuned on the real layouts, with the real-capture M-A-P, once those numbers exist. If they do not exist at the final cut, say instead: "Fine-tuning the detector on real frames is the next step, and the layouts for it are recorded."]


Scene 12. Close.

ON SCREEN: The planner's text block, full screen. Then the fields of the object record. Then the title card with the line: Code built with Claude Code under the author's design and review.

So this is what comes out at the end: a short list of facts, from two real cameras, that a planner can start from. The object format behind it is a fixed contract. Fields can be added but never removed, because my later projects will build on it.

Two cameras do see more than one. But what I learned is that the second camera matters most as a witness. It is what tells you when the first one is wrong.

The code was built with Claude Code, under my design and review. Thank you for watching.
